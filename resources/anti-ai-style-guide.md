# Anti-AI Style Guide

> A field guide for writing things that don't sound like AI wrote them.

> reference panel, or just check your own drafts against it before you

## The bar in 2026

The standard isn't "did you use AI." The standard is **"can I tell you used AI."**

If a reader can't tell, the work is yours.
If they can tell, the work reads as the model's, and they wonder why you didn't bother to finish it.

Use AI freely. Just strip its fingerprints from the final draft.

---

## Banned phrases - never use

These are AI default vocabulary. Real humans almost never say them in casual writing.

**Opening filler:**
- "It's important to note that..."
- "In today's world..."
- "Let's explore..."
- "Let's break it down"
- "First and foremost"
- "Last but not least"

**Closing filler:**
- "In conclusion..."
- "To sum up..."
- "All things considered..."
- "Ultimately, what matters is..."

**Marketing-speak:**
- "Cutting-edge"
- "Game-changer"
- "Take it to the next level"
- "Unlock the potential"
- "Synergy"
- "Leverage" (used as a verb on anything that isn't a fulcrum)
- "Ecosystem" (used metaphorically)
- "Landscape" (the abstract kind)

**Purple prose:**
- "Tapestry"
- "Realm"
- "Beacon"
- "Journey" (as a metaphor)
- "Embark"
- "Delve"
- "Underscore"
- "Harness"
- "Illuminate"
- "Facilitate"
- "Bolster"

**Filler nouns:**
- "Nuance" (especially "the nuance is...")
- "Myriad"
- "Plethora"
- "Panoply"
- "Tapestry of [anything]"

If three or more of these show up in the same paragraph, AI was involved. Strip them and rewrite the sentence the way you'd explain the same thing to a friend.

## Structural tells - the patterns AI loves

Phrases are easy to spot. Patterns are harder but more reliable.

### The "Not X - it's Y" reframing

> "It's not just a tool - it's an extension of your team."
> "Not a feature - a philosophy."
> "It's not about the destination, it's about the journey."

This pattern showed up in GPT-3 training data and never left. Real humans occasionally write a "not X but Y." They don't do it three times a paragraph. AI does.

If you see this pattern more than once in a piece of writing, AI was involved.

### Three-item lists

AI loves a triplet.
- "Fast, cheap, and reliable."
- "Bold, decisive, and ready to act."
- "Innovative, scalable, and future-proof."

Humans use lists too, but the lengths vary. Sometimes two items. Sometimes four. Sometimes a stray fifth thrown in as an afterthought. AI defaults to three almost every time.

### Uniform sentence length

Read a paragraph aloud. If every sentence takes about the same number of breaths, AI wrote it (or someone trained on how-to-write-essays guides).

Real prose has rhythm. Short sentence. Then a longer one that builds and rolls until it lands. Then a fragment. Like that.

### Uniform paragraph length

Same problem at the paragraph level. AI tends to produce paragraphs that all hover around the same word count. Real writing has a one-line paragraph next to a chunky 200-word one because that's how the thoughts came out.

### Rhetorical question, then immediate answer

> "What does this mean for your business? It means..."

Nobody talks like that. Real questions hang. AI fills them in instantly because its training rewards completeness.

### Starting too many sentences with the subject

> "Companies need to think strategically. They should consider their long-term goals. They must also evaluate..."

Every sentence opens with a noun. Real writing varies the starts - prepositional phrases, subordinate clauses, fragments, dialogue.

### The trailing modifier

> "...making it a powerful tool for modern teams."
> "...creating endless possibilities for innovation."
> "...delivering value at every stage of the customer journey."

That trailing phrase is AI's way of stuffing in keywords for completeness. Real writers cut these because they don't add information.

## The em dash

The single most reliable AI tell in 2026 is the em dash (—).

Not the en dash (–). Not the hyphen (-). The actual em dash.

AI training data is heavy on edited journalism, polished essays, and Wikipedia, all of which use em dashes properly. AI learned that em dashes signal "good writing" and now sprinkles them everywhere.

Most humans either:
- Don't know how to type an em dash on their keyboard
- Use a regular dash (-) for the same purpose
- Use parentheses or commas instead

If you see em dashes in casual writing - emails, LinkedIn comments, tweets, blog comments, cold outreach - AI was almost certainly involved.

**The fix:** when prompting AI, tell it explicitly to use regular dashes (-) instead of em dashes (—). The model will comply for that response. It won't volunteer the change otherwise.

## Tone rules - what humans do that AI defaults away from

- Be direct. Have opinions. Take sides.
- Use contractions always (don't, you're, it's, can't, won't).
- Swear when it fits. AI scrubs profanity by default.
- Use "I" freely. AI avoids first person; humans use it constantly.
- No false balance. If one side is obviously right, say so.
- No wishy-washy hedging. Cut "some might say...", "it could be argued...", "perspectives vary." Just say it.
- Concrete over abstract. Real examples beat frameworks.
- Use fragments. "Kinda weird." "Wild." "Anyway."
- Go on tangents occasionally. "Oh, actually that reminds me-" then come back.
- Add subtle humour, sarcasm, dry observations.

## Spelling - pick a dialect and commit

If you're Australian or British: colour, favourite, behaviour, organised, realise, centre, defence, licence, analyse, customise.

If you're American: color, favorite, behavior, etc.

What gives AI away: mixing them in the same document. AI defaults to American but will switch if asked, then drift back partway through. Pick one and be consistent.

## The 17-point self-check

Run this before you send any writing you want to be taken seriously. After a few times it takes about 30 seconds.

1. Any banned phrases?
2. Every paragraph the same length?
3. Every sentence the same length?
4. Too many sentences starting with the subject?
5. Hedging instead of having an opinion?
6. Bolding random words?
7. Too many three-item lists?
8. "Not X - it's Y" pattern anywhere?
9. Semicolons where a comma or full stop would do?
10. Sentences ending with "...making it X" / "...creating Y"?
11. Does it sound like something ChatGPT would say?
12. Spelling consistent with how you actually spell?
13. Using "I" enough?
14. Any personality, humour, or opinion - or is it flat?
15. Any tangents, asides, or "wait actually" moments?
16. Too polished? Real messages are a bit rough.
17. Any em dashes (—)? Replace with normal dash (-).

## How to use this with an AI tool

Drop this whole file into your AI tool's system prompt or context. Then prompt with something like:

> "Write a [thing]. Apply the anti-AI style guide. No banned phrases, no em dashes, no Not-X-it's-Y patterns, vary sentence length, use first person, sound like a human at the pub explaining the thing."

The output will still need a final pass from you - models drift. But it'll be 80% closer to human-readable than the default output.

---

## Why this matters

Cold outreach: AI-default writing gets filtered as noise inside the first sentence. Human-flavoured writing gets read.

Marketing copy: AI-default copy reads as fungible. Human-flavoured copy builds brand voice.

Cover letters and applications: AI tells signal you didn't bother. Human tells signal effort and care.

Internal documentation: AI tells obscure intent. Human writing makes the "why" of decisions clear.

The cost of sounding like AI scales with how much the reader's trust matters.
