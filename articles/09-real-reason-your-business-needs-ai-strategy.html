The Real Reason Your Business Needs an AI Strategy (And It's Not Efficiency)

By Cameron J. Moir  ·  Streamables.live
//

Every conversation about AI for business eventually lands on efficiency. Automate the repetitive tasks. Speed up the slow processes. Reduce headcount on low-value work. Save time. Save money. The ROI calculator shows green and everyone nods.

That's all real. Efficiency gains from AI are genuine and meaningful for most businesses. But efficiency is the wrong reason to develop an AI strategy, and businesses that approach it purely through that lens are going to miss what actually matters.

The real reason to think seriously about AI right now is competitive positioning.

Not in the sense of "adopt AI or get left behind" - that's the panic version of the argument and it leads to bad decisions. In the more specific sense that your competitors are going to have capabilities in two to three years that you either prepared for or you didn't, and the gap between prepared and unprepared is going to be wide.

What capabilities specifically?

The ability to synthesise large amounts of information quickly. Right now, competitive intelligence is slow - you can keep track of your direct competitors, maybe a few adjacent ones, and the major industry publications. In two years, a business with a properly set-up AI research capability can monitor the entire competitive landscape continuously, surface relevant signals in real time, and brief decision-makers with synthesised analysis rather than raw feeds. That's a genuine capability gap.

The ability to personalise at a scale that was previously impossible. Mass-market businesses have always had to trade off personalisation against scale. AI changes that. A restaurant group with 200,000 loyalty members can deliver genuinely personalised communications and recommendations based on individual behaviour patterns, not just demographic segments. A services business can customise proposals, scope, and delivery based on detailed client histories. The personalisation isn't a gimmick - it produces measurably better outcomes in conversion, retention, and satisfaction.

The ability to surface non-obvious insights from your own operational data. Most businesses are sitting on years of data they've never properly analysed because analysis was expensive and slow. AI makes it cheap and fast. The businesses that start running this analysis now are going to understand their own operations at a level of detail that creates real decision-making advantages.

The ability to move faster. Not just on individual tasks but on the whole cycle from insight to decision to execution. When research, analysis, drafting, and iteration all compress by 40-60%, the business that has that capability and the one that doesn't are operating at different speeds. Over three years, that compounds into a serious structural advantage.

The businesses I worry about aren't the ones that have no AI strategy. They're the ones that have a shallow AI strategy - they've adopted a tool here and there, they've maybe put ChatGPT into their workflow for drafting, and they think they've addressed it.

Shallow adoption is almost as bad as no adoption because it creates false confidence. You're not getting the real advantage, but you think you are, so you're not preparing for what's coming.

Real AI strategy involves a few things that shallow adoption doesn't touch.

Data infrastructure. AI tools are only as useful as the data they can access. If your data is fragmented across systems that don't talk to each other, you can't build the capabilities that actually matter. Sorting out your data infrastructure is unglamorous and takes time, but it's the foundation everything else builds on. Businesses that do this in the next two years will be significantly ahead of those that put it off.

Building internal capability, not just buying tools. Tools change fast. A vendor you depend on today might not exist in three years, or might change its pricing model, or might be acquired and pivoted. The businesses that develop internal understanding of AI - people who know enough to evaluate tools, manage implementations, and adapt when things change - are more resilient than the ones who just buy SaaS subscriptions and hope the vendor stays relevant.

Thinking about AI and your people together, not separately. The standard framing is "AI will automate some jobs." That's true in some contexts. But the more useful framing for most businesses is "AI changes what's valuable in a person." The skills that made someone excellent five years ago aren't necessarily the same skills that make someone excellent now. A customer service person who's good at handling complex emotional situations and navigating difficult conversations is more valuable with AI tools than without. A content creator who's good at ideas and strategy but slow at production becomes much faster. Understanding how AI changes the value of different human contributions in your specific context is part of strategy, not just a footnote.

One specific thing I'd push back on is the idea that small businesses need to wait for AI to mature before they invest seriously in it. That's backwards. Large businesses are going to adopt AI tools regardless - they have the budget, the teams, and the vendor relationships. The window where a small or medium-sized business can develop genuine AI capability before the big players fully systematise it is right now, not later.

The advantage of being a small business is that you can move fast and adapt. The disadvantage is that you don't have the resources to do everything. So prioritise ruthlessly. One or two genuinely high-value applications, implemented properly, with someone who owns them - that beats ten shallow implementations every time.

The efficiency gains are the easy win. The competitive positioning is the real game. Best to start thinking about both.
