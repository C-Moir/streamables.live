Hospitality Is a Data Goldmine Nobody's Mining

By Cameron J. Moir  ·  Streamables.live
//

Twenty years working in and around commercial kitchens and hospitality operations leaves you with a specific appreciation for how much information flows through a venue every single day - and how almost none of it gets captured usefully.

Cover counts, table turn times, dish send rates by section, wastage by ingredient by shift, staff performance patterns, weather correlation with booking volumes, the relationship between your bar spend per cover and your food spend per cover, the difference in average spend between bookings taken online versus phone versus walk-in. It's all there. It happens every service. And for most venues, it disappears.

Not because operators don't care about it. Because capturing it has historically required either expensive integrated systems or dedicated admin time that most operations don't have.

The grill section tracker we built for a grill-heavy Brisbane venue is a simple example of what capturing this data actually looks like in practice. A spreadsheet - later a digital form - that records what goes out of the grill section by shift, cross-referenced against prep quantities and end-of-shift inventory. Takes the section chef three minutes at the end of service to complete.

Over eight weeks, that data tells you things you couldn't see before. Which nights are your grill section running the highest send volumes. Where the gap is between prep quantity and actual send - which tells you either about wastage or about prep accuracy. Which staff configurations produce the highest section output. Whether your Wednesday night send rate justifies the prep quantity you're currently doing or whether you're over-prepping by 20% every week.

None of those insights require sophisticated analytics. They require the data to exist in a consistent format across enough services to be meaningful. That's the part that was missing.

The kitchen resource management system extends this across the whole operation. Once you have consistent section-level tracking, you can start correlating across sections - whether your pastry output timing is affecting your main course send rate, whether your front-of-house order pacing is creating grill section bottlenecks on specific nights, whether your table assignment patterns are causing service timing issues that don't show up in the individual section data.

This is operational intelligence that most venues literally cannot access right now because the data points don't exist or aren't connected. It's not about having a smarter operator - the existing operators often have the intuitions already. It's about having data that confirms or challenges those intuitions with something more reliable than gut feel.

The venues that get this right start making better decisions faster. Which menu items to cut because the margin and the kitchen complexity aren't justified by the cover volume. Which staff configurations to standardise because the data shows they outperform. Which shifts need prep quantity adjustments because the actual send rates don't match the prep assumptions.

The AI layer comes in when you have enough consistent data to start doing something predictive with it.

Predictive ordering is the most immediately valuable application. A kitchen that's tracked its actual ingredient usage across 12 months - including seasonal patterns, event correlations, weather effects on booking volumes, day-of-week patterns by menu category - can use that data to generate purchase orders that are significantly more accurate than the chef's experience-based estimate. Not because the AI is smarter than the chef. Because it has access to more data than any person can hold in working memory simultaneously.

Industry estimates put venue food wastage somewhere between 8-15% of food spend - a figure that varies significantly by kitchen type and operational discipline, but consistent with what I've seen across the operations I've worked with. In a kitchen doing $12,000 a week on food, bringing that down by half - achievable with good tracking and predictive ordering - is worth $25,000-$35,000 a year. That's a meaningful number for any operation.

There's a broader point about hospitality as an industry that I think is worth making.

Hospitality generates an enormous amount of data about human behaviour. What people eat, when they eat it, who they eat it with, how their food choices correlate with other signals about their preferences and lifestyle. The industry has generally been terrible at capturing and using this data for its own benefit - the value has mostly been captured by third-party booking platforms, delivery aggregators, and review sites that sit on top of venues and extract margin while the venues do the work.

The venues that own their data - their customer data, their operational data, their purchase and sales data in consistent usable formats - are in a fundamentally different position to those that don't. They can make better decisions. They can build direct customer relationships that aren't mediated by a platform taking 15-30% of every transaction. They can understand their own operations at a level of specificity that drives real improvement.

Data ownership in hospitality isn't a tech conversation. It's a business fundamentals conversation. The information about how your business operates belongs to you. It should be working for you, not leaking out to platforms that are charging you to access data about your own customers.

The combination of operational systems, section tracking, and data capture that we've been building out isn't primarily a tech story. It's about giving operators back the intelligence that was always being generated by their businesses but never captured in a usable form.

Twenty years of watching good operators make decisions based on intuition and experience when they could have been making them based on intuition and data - that's what this is trying to fix.

The data was always there. We just started writing it down.
