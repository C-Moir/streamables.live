Mistakes, Captured Honestly

By Cameron J. Moir  ·  Streamables.live
//

Earlier this week I shipped a build log on streamables.live - a public article documenting exactly what landed in ClipTips, including the mistakes. Three days into writing it I noticed something useful: the act of knowing the article was going public forced me to capture mistakes I would otherwise have ignored.

The session log this post is meta-talking about lives at docs/sessions/2026-04-29.md in the ClipTips repo. It’s 162 lines of "what I shipped today + the friction I hit + what carries forward." The "what I shipped" part is easy. The "friction I hit" part is the one that gets dropped from most engineering write-ups, because nobody wants to publish their mistakes.

I think that’s wrong. Documentation that hides mistakes is documentation that doesn’t teach. So this is a short post about the discipline of capturing mistakes in writing, why public visibility matters, and how it actually changes the quality of the engineering itself.

The mistakes from this week

Three captured in the session log:

Anchored on Wav2Lip too long. The original V1.5 lipsync plan referenced Wav2Lip (2020 model, IIIT Hyderabad, research-only license). When the conversation turned to "what about lipsync video," I parroted the parent plan’s framing and recommended forking Wav2Lip. Wrong call - Tencent’s MuseTalk shipped in 2024, MIT-licensed, way better quality, real-time on V100. The lesson: web-search current state-of-the-art before quoting old plan docs. State of the art moves monthly in this space.

Andrew/Brittany rename revert. Mid-session I started swapping demo voice IDs from Harry/Sally to Andrew/Brittany. Two of the Edit operations failed silently because surrounding code had drifted, leaving a partial state with four TypeScript errors. Spent ~30 minutes recovering. The lesson: when an Edit doesn’t take, stop and re-read the actual file state before firing more edits. Don’t pile changes on top of a broken intermediate state.

Inflated cost estimates. First three iterations of margin tables used $0.42-0.70/min AI cost figures. After verifying real provider pricing, the actual cost was $0.23/min - about half what I’d been telling myself. The lesson: verify provider pricing first, build the cost model second. Not the other way round.

None of these are catastrophic. None of them are flattering either. All three would have been easy to leave out of the public article and just quietly file as "lessons learned" in private notes nobody reads. I left them in.

Why public mistakes are the most useful documentation

The argument against publishing mistakes: it makes you look bad, costs credibility, gives ammunition to critics. The argument is wrong on all three counts.

It doesn’t make you look bad. It makes you look like someone who actually works. Engineers reading public build logs are themselves engineers - they recognise the texture of real work. A build log without mistakes reads as marketing fluff. A build log with mistakes reads as a real human shipping a real product. Trust goes up, not down.

It doesn’t cost credibility. The opposite. Reading a write-up where someone confidently asserts they made the right architectural call from day one, I assume they’re lying or they haven’t done enough work to discover what was wrong with their plan. Public mistakes are a credibility signal because they’re what self-aware practitioners produce.

It doesn’t arm critics. The mistakes critics latch onto are the ones you tried to hide. If you publish them yourself, you control the narrative. The Wav2Lip-then-MuseTalk pivot in the article reads as "got the right answer eventually." Hidden, it would have looked like a hidden flaw. Same fact, different framing - and writers control the framing.

The accountability loop

Here’s the part that actually matters for engineering quality: knowing the mistakes will be public makes you make fewer of them.

Concrete example from this week. After the Wav2Lip pivot, the next time I needed to recommend a piece of tech (a Sieve API contract decision, ElevenLabs Flash v2.5 vs Multilingual v2), I web-searched first. I’d been about to anchor on what the existing plan said. The voice in my head was "if I get this wrong it goes in the session log next time." I checked. Turned out the recommendation was right but the data was different from what I assumed.

The accountability loop: knowing future-you will write down what you got wrong forces present-you to do the work.

This is the same mechanism that makes pair programming improve code quality. Not because the second person catches more bugs (they don’t, statistically), but because the writer’s code is being seen so it gets written more carefully. Public documentation creates the same loop, just with a delay.

Private notes don’t create the loop. Slack threads don’t. Even internal-team-only Confluence doesn’t. The loop only fires when the writing is destined for an audience the writer cares about being seen by.

The session log format

The format I’m using - one markdown file per build session, dropped in docs/sessions/YYYY-MM-DD.md - is light enough that maintaining it doesn’t slow shipping down. Sections look like:

  • Summary - one line. "Phase 5f closed + Phase A latency optimisations + V1.5 free-tier prep."
  • Production state delta - a small table comparing start-of-day to end-of-day on the metrics that matter for that build.
  • What shipped - commit SHAs + one-line subjects.
  • Decisions made - design calls + their reasoning.
  • What didn’t go well - the mistakes section. The whole point.
  • Verifiable end state - what someone reading later can check to confirm the work landed.
  • What’s outstanding - what I didn’t finish + why.

~150 lines per file. ~30 minutes to write at end of day. Pays back the next time I write an article (because the article is mostly transcription of the log) or the next time I need to remember why I made a decision two months ago.

The implicit deal

Solo founders especially benefit from public mistakes-capture because they’re the only person who can audit their own work. There’s no senior engineer reading a PR. No retrospective with the team. No code review. The session log + public article workflow is the closest thing to peer review available when you’re working alone.

The deal you’re making with future-you is: I will tell you the truth about what happened today, including the parts that make me look bad, in exchange for getting to keep working on this product without lying to myself about why it’s where it is.

Companies that fail in obvious ways usually had a moment, three months earlier, when someone could have written down what was actually broken and chose not to because the truth was uncomfortable. The session log practice doesn’t prevent failure. It just removes the "I didn’t see this coming" excuse from the failure mode.

What you can copy

If you’re building solo, or in a small team, and you don’t already have a session-log practice:

  • Pick a folder. docs/sessions/ or journal/ or build-log/. Doesn’t matter.
  • Pick a filename pattern. YYYY-MM-DD.md works. So does YYYY-WW-week.md if you log weekly instead of daily.
  • Pick a fixed section list. Mine has 7 sections (above). Use them every time.
  • Write at end of session, not mid-session. Mid-session interrupts flow. End-of-session is reflection.
  • Include the friction sections. Skip them and you’ve built a marketing asset, not a documentation asset.
  • Commit them to the repo. Public if you can stomach it; private repo if you can’t. Either way, version-controlled.

Adoption cost: 30 minutes per session. Compounding return: every future article, every future onboarding doc, every future "why did we do this" question gets answered from the log instead of memory.
